📶 Get connected before you land

Many Western apps and sites (Google, Maps, Gmail, WhatsApp, Facebook, Instagram, X) are blocked in mainland China. Set this up before you arrive — VPN apps and their websites are also often blocked once you're inside the firewall.

Install and test your VPN app while still on home Wi-Fi. Choose one with dedicated China servers/protocols (e.g. built for bypassing the Great Firewall) rather than a generic VPN. Have a second one as backup.

SIM / data options

  • eSIM (e.g. Alipay's own data plans, or international eSIM providers) — easiest, activates before you land.
  • Physical China SIM from China Unicom/Mobile/Telecom at the airport (needs passport).
  • Roaming on your home SIM — simplest but pricier; check if your carrier blocks the sites above anyway.

💳 China is (almost) cashless

Most shops, taxis, and street stalls expect a QR-code scan via Alipay or WeChat Pay. Foreign physical cards (Visa/Mastercard) are accepted at big hotels and some chains, but not much else. Set up mobile pay before you go out.

Setting up Alipay with a foreign card

1Download Alipay, sign up with your phone number.
2Go to Card → Add Card and link a Visa/Mastercard/Amex/Discover/Diners/JCB — this uses Alipay's "Tour Pass" for international visitors.
3Verify your identity with your passport (photo + selfie).
4Top up a small balance or let it charge your card directly per transaction (small service fee may apply above a monthly free quota).
5To pay: open Alipay → Scan (or show your Pay/Collect QR code for the cashier to scan) → confirm amount.

WeChat Pay has a similar "foreign card binding" flow under Wallet → Add Card. Setting up both is safer since not every vendor accepts both apps.

Good to know

  • Carry some cash (¥) as backup — a few small vendors, markets, and rural areas are cash-only or app-setup can glitch.
  • UnionPay ATMs at banks/airports let you withdraw cash from foreign cards.
  • Tipping is not customary/expected in mainland China.
  • Bargaining is common at markets, not in regular shops, restaurants, or malls.
  • Tell your home bank you're travelling, so foreign-currency top-ups aren't flagged as fraud.

🚄 High-speed rail

  • Book via the official 12306 app/site or through Trip.com (English UI, small booking fee).
  • Your passport is your ticket — tickets are linked to it, no need to print (in most cities you can tap your passport at the gate; some stations still require collecting a paper ticket first).
  • Arrive 30–45 min early: security screening is like an airport's.
  • Seat classes: Second Class, First Class, Business Class (fastest boarding, best seats).

🚇 City metro & buses

  • Open Alipay or WeChat → Transit QR Code (often called "乘车码") and scan at the gate — works in most major cities.
  • Alternatively buy a rechargeable transit card at any station, or pay cash for single tickets from machines (English available).
  • Use Amap/Baidu Maps for routes — bilingual signage is generally strong in big cities.

🚕 Taxis & ride-hailing

  • DiDi (switch app to English / "DiDi International") is the most reliable way to get a ride without speaking Chinese — set pickup/drop-off pins on the map.
  • Street taxis are cheap but drivers rarely speak English — have your destination written in Chinese characters (screenshot from maps) to show them.
  • Airport transfers: Airport Express trains, official taxi queues, or DiDi/pre-booked transfers. Avoid unofficial touts offering rides in the terminal.

🛂 Visa basics

Rules change by nationality and port of entry — always confirm on the Chinese embassy/consulate website for your country before booking.

Common visa types

  • L (Tourist) — sightseeing, visiting family/friends.
  • M (Business) — trade/commercial activity, needs an invitation letter.
  • F (Visit) — exchanges, study tours, non-commercial visits.
  • Z (Work), X (Study) — need a sponsoring employer/school.

Visa-free transit (144 hours)

Many nationalities can transit through eligible cities/regions visa-free for up to 144 hours (6 days) if continuing to a third country/region, entering and exiting through designated ports. Check the current eligible-country list and ports before relying on this.

Some nationalities qualify for unilateral visa-free entry for short stays (policy has expanded in recent years) — check your country specifically, it may save you the whole application.

Typical documents needed

  • Passport valid 6+ months with blank visa pages.
  • Passport photo (per embassy spec).
  • Flight itinerary (round-trip/onward).
  • Hotel bookings for your whole stay.
  • Invitation letter (for business/family visits).
  • Proof of funds, in some cases.

After you arrive

If staying at a private residence (not a hotel), you're generally required to register with the local police station within 24 hours. Hotels handle this automatically at check-in.

Before you travel

  • Save your embassy/consulate's emergency contact number in your phone — look it up on your government's travel advisory site for China.
  • Get travel insurance that covers medical evacuation.
  • Keep photos of your passport, visa, and insurance policy stored offline (and emailed to yourself).
  • Share your itinerary with someone back home.

If you need a hospital

Ask your hotel concierge to call ahead and, if possible, send someone with you — few staff at public hospitals speak English. Large cities have international clinics (e.g. hospital "VIP"/international wings) that are more used to foreign patients, at a higher price. Keep all receipts for insurance claims.
